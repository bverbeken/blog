I'm in the car. The radio is playing what later turns out to be Horn Concerto No. 4 in E flat, by Mozart. I'd love to be able to write music like that, I think. And that beautiful music gets me thinking.

Mozart wrote his music in a time when the rules of music were very strict. He also lived in a time when artists weren't judged on their originality. Today, I think we confuse creativity with originality, and require artists to do new things. Mozart, in his way, did that too, but his way of being original was a lot subtler. Like starting his 40th symphony with the accompaniment instead of the melody.

So Mozart followed strict rules, and hid his genius in subtle novelties. My next thought is, naturally: an AI could write this music.

Now, I'm not a Mozart connoisseur by any means, and I may be kicking Mozart connoisseurs in the proverbial notes with this, but: *suppose*, just for a second and for the sake of argument, that an AI is actually capable of writing music just like Wolfgang. Following the same rules, introducing the same subtle creative touches. So well that musicologists across the globe would agree, after thorough research, that it's a genuine W.A. Mozart score. Suppose.

What would that do to our perception of the music? Is the artistic value of the music in the notes alone? Or is it also in the person who wrote them, and when? Does context matter?

### Han van Meegeren
Han van Meegeren (1889–1947), a Dutch painter, was through with critics dismissing his work as old-fashioned and derivative, and decided to paint and sell forged Vermeer paintings. He would buy real 17th-century paintings, scrape off most of the paint and work on genuinely old frames and canvases. He would paint biblical scenes, not the domestic scenes Vermeer is famous for, tricking experts into believing Vermeer had an unknown early religious period. In short, he went the whole nine yards. To the point that Abraham Bredius, the leading Vermeer expert of the time, called *The Supper at Emmaus* (1937) "a masterpiece", and a museum bought it for about 520,000 guilders.

In 1945 he was arrested for selling a "Vermeer" to Hermann Göring, and confessed he had painted them all. He's remembered as a folk hero.

But here's the kicker: since 1945, almost all experts have agreed that Van Meegeren's Vermeer fakes are "heavy-handed and awkward", that they "look nothing like Vermeer", and that the painted people "looked like 1930s film stars".

### Fritz Kreisler
Fritz Kreisler (1875–1962) was a violinist and composer, born in Vienna. At 7, he became the youngest student ever admitted to the Vienna Conservatory, and eventually became one of the most famous violinists of the 20th century.

He, too, faked it. He wrote short pieces in an 18th-century style, mostly as material for his own recitals, and published them as a series called "Classical Manuscripts", saying they were pieces by Pugnani, Vivaldi, Couperin and others, which he had found in old libraries and monasteries across Europe. The best-known is *Praeludium and Allegro*, published "in the style of Pugnani".

In 1935, a researcher started digging into the pieces, and Kreisler admitted they were his.

Some critics were furious, and a public argument in the press followed. Kreisler reportedly replied: "The name changes, the value remains."

To this day, Kreisler's Classical Manuscripts are still played, now under his name.

### Joshua Bell
Joshua Bell (born 1967) is one of the best-known classical violinists alive. He plays the "Gibson ex-Huberman" Stradivarius from 1713, which he bought in 2001 for $4M.

Writer Gene Weingarten (The Washington Post) wanted to see if people would notice great music in an ordinary place.

And so, on January 12, 2007, during the morning rush hour, Bell - wearing jeans and a baseball cap - played his Stradivarius in the L'Enfant Plaza metro station in Washington, D.C. His repertoire consisted of demanding classical works (Schubert, Bach and more). Everything was filmed with a hidden camera.

The result:

* Some 1,100 people walked by.
* Seven stopped for a minute or more.
* One woman recognized him and tipped him $20.
* Not a single soul applauded when he finished a piece.
* Children often wanted to stop and listen, but their parents pulled them along every time, without exception.

In 2014, Bell played again in a subway station (Washington's Union Station). This time, however, the concert was announced, and a large crowd came.

### Back to Mozart
These three stories tell the same tale. Looking just at the paint, Van Meegeren's fake Vermeers are very well-executed paintings. Same for Kreisler's Classical Manuscripts: they're, quite simply put, good music.

But they are not Vermeers. They are not Vivaldis. And that's what made the experts change their mind: once they knew the truth about who created the work, they suddenly saw awkward paintings and 1930s film stars.

The public, on the other hand, didn't care. Van Meegeren became a folk hero for fooling Göring, and Kreisler's music is still played.

But the public doesn't *only* look at paint or listen to notes. Almost nobody stopped for Joshua Bell in the subway. Fair, it was rush hour. But when people knew who was playing, they came in crowds.

Back in the car, my first thought was "this is beautiful music". The second was "this might be Mozart", and that made me like the music more. After the last notes, the presenter confirmed it was Mozart. I guess I'm not immune to the name either. And if an AI had written it? I *think* that would take away a lot of the value, for me. But I'm not sure it should.

The question I'm left with is: what does this mean for music or art made today?
